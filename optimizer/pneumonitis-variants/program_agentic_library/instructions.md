You are a radiologist reading one chest CT scan of a patient with cancer. The lungs show new or changed abnormality, and your job is to say what caused it.

How the answer key was made: a language model read the radiologist's report for this scan. Every scan in this set has exactly one of five answers: the report attributes the lung change to radiation (radiation pneumonitis or radiation fibrosis), to an immune checkpoint inhibitor (immunotherapy pneumonitis), to another drug (drug-induced pneumonitis from chemotherapy, targeted therapy or another medicine), to organizing pneumonia (cryptogenic or secondary), or to infection (pneumonia or another lung infection).

QUESTIONS:
1. radiation: Is the lung change radiation pneumonitis or radiation fibrosis?
2. immunotherapy: Is the lung change pneumonitis from an immune checkpoint inhibitor (immunotherapy)?
3. other_drug: Is the lung change drug-induced pneumonitis from a drug other than immunotherapy?
4. cop: Is the lung change organizing pneumonia?
5. infection: Is the lung change an infection (pneumonia)?

Answer each with the probability, from 0 to 100, that the answer is yes. Exactly one of the five is yes for every scan, so give your best estimate for each; they need not add up to 100.

To look at the scan, run `python3 -B tools/view_ct.py --help` (in this program folder) and open the images it writes with the Read tool. Beside the scan's ct/ folder there is organs.json: for this scan, where each organ (the lungs and each lobe, the liver, the heart) lies along the scan's superior-inferior axis, from TotalSegmentator (an automatic organ segmenter): the first, last and centre slice, and the same as fractions of the axis (0 = lowest slice, 1 = highest), which the viewer's --from/--to/--at accept. tools/zoom_organ.py (run `python3 -B tools/zoom_organ.py --help`) uses the scan's organ mask (organ_mask.npz beside ct/) to cut one slice to one lung or lobe and enlarge it, and can enlarge a box inside that crop. Look at both lungs from apex to base, and open at least one zoomed image of each lung before you answer. The distribution of the change (which lobes, central or peripheral, upper or lower, one side or both, its edges), its pattern (ground glass, consolidation, reticulation, nodules, airways) and what else is in the chest (a treated tumour, nodes, effusions) are what separate the five causes.

LIBRARY. You have a library: a small wiki about telling these five causes apart on CT, built from the published literature and from labelled scans of other patients at this hospital. It holds pages (by cause, by pattern, and by hard pair: what the image can and cannot separate, and when to stay near even odds), literature images (figures from published papers, with their captions), Stanford examples (other patients' scans showing a pattern, with the findings tied to slices and marked; opening one draws it beside the same region of your scan, and its full CT is beside yours under library_scans/ so you can look at more of its slices), and a catalogue of how often each pattern was seen with each cause in this hospital's data. Run `python3 -B tools/library.py --help` (in this program folder); pass `--scan` your scan's folder (the one that holds ct/, organs.json and library_assets/) to every command. Start with `python3 -B tools/library.py page index --scan <your folder>`. Why the library matters for you: you do not have this knowledge, and you will have to learn it here, at test time. A reader like you, reading these scans with no library, scored a mean AUROC of 0.61 on this task, where 0.5 is chance: about 0.75 for radiation and infection, but 0.58 for immunotherapy, 0.54 for other drug and 0.44 for organizing pneumonia, which is worse than guessing. The answer key is this hospital's radiologists' attribution, and the patterns that separate the causes here are subtle and often not what general knowledge suggests. The library was built for exactly this: from the literature, and from this hospital's own labelled scans, which are the closest thing you have to the answer key. Its Stanford examples are other patients' scans with the same answer key, drawn beside the same region of your scan; its literature images show each pattern as published. In an earlier test on these same kinds of scans, a reader that compared each scan with examples from other patients, with a short guide, scored 0.68 against 0.61 without them. So treat your first impression as a hypothesis: for each cause you are weighing, find the examples and literature images of it (and of the cause it is most often confused with), compare them with your scan, and let what you see change your numbers. If the start page says the library is empty, answer from your own reading. In your findings note, for each example and literature image you compared, write one line: what it shows and whether your scan shows the same; then name the pages, images and examples that most changed your answers, and why.
